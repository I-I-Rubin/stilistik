# Introduction.

## The Task and Divisions of Stylistics.

§ 1 _If_ we survey, in its broadest outlines, the history of writing Latin from the time when cultivated literary Latin ceased to be a living language[^1a], two distinct main periods present themselves. The first extends to the revival of classical studies in the culturally developed countries of the West; the second reaches down to our own day. Each is divided into further periods. In the _first_ period, Latin is the language of the Church, of scholarship, and of practical affairs, both public and private (medieval written Latin), and forms the international bond between the peoples of the West. As the universal language of Christendom in its religious and political life, Latin is something that must necessarily be learnt at school. Consequently, it is also treated as something that has virtually no value beyond meeting this need. Where the language's inherited vocabulary does not meet it, violence is done to the language: it must submit to being adapted and moulded for the services it is wanted to perform. In <!-- PDF_PAGE: 2 --> itself it is accorded little importance, and thus its distinctive character is neither particularly studied nor respected. Yet distinctions must be made in the use of Latin, both between periods and between the fields it serves. During the transitional period, which begins at different times in the different former Roman provinces, but generally in the final decades of the sixth century, and ends in the first half of the ninth, the dominance of vernacular Latin (vulgar medieval Latin) extends even to the highest and most cultivated circles in the countries of the former <span lang="la"><i>imperium Romanum</i></span>. From the middle of the ninth to about the middle of the thirteenth century, literary Latin has the character of a reproduction of the literary Latin that had died out, a fruit of the efforts towards a literary renaissance made by Charlemagne and the Carolingian age. This Latin stood in opposition to the Romance vernaculars, which had arisen from “variously developed forms of Vulgar Latin” and were increasingly acquiring their own individual character, and to the distinctive Germanic vernaculars, although it could not entirely escape their influence. From about the middle of the thirteenth century, traditional literary Latin begins, in not a few works, to be distorted almost beyond recognition. Yet within the three stages of medieval Latin, the fields in which Latin is used stand in different relations to Roman literature, although all are subject to the influence of their time. The Latinity of the language of practical affairs, composed from the outset of elements of literary and Vulgar Latin, departed from literary Latin in proportion as Romance or Germanic linguistic elements gained ground. The language of the Church, rooted not only in biblical Latin [^2a] but also maintaining an unbroken continuity with the language as it had developed in Christian and secular literature before the collapse of the supports of ancient culture, always knew how to find, in its liturgies and hymns, the most fitting and dignified expression for the world of thought<!-- PDF_PAGE: 3; original word break: Gedanken-|kreis --> of the <span lang="la">'una sancta catholica et apostolica ecclesia'</span>[^3a]. After the restoration of the grammatical correctness of literary Latin in the Carolingian age, sacred and secular scholarship retained the character of the literary Latin handed down to it. It did so under the influence of able monastic, cathedral, and collegiate schools, which preserved or revived elements of ancient culture[^3b], and through writers as accomplished in their command of form as they were gifted and learned, who could even approach classical models[^3c]. This character faded more and more, however, as medieval thought took its distinctive shape in scholasticism[^3d], and eventually gave way to a Latin that only faintly recalled its ancient origin. If the otherworldly<!-- PDF_PAGE: 4; original word break: trans-|zendentale --> and ascetic character of scholastic learning had already produced indifference towards ancient culture and ancient forms of expression in general, this indifference increased still further when scholarship lost itself in <span lang="la"><i>dumeta</i></span> and <span lang="la"><i>spinae disserendi</i></span>, while the remaining thirst for knowledge sought satisfaction in slavishly literal translations from Greek, and still more in translations from Arabic into Latin whose barbarism reached the point of unintelligibility.

§ 2 The revival of classical studies in the fifteenth century also begins a _new_ epoch in the history of writing Latin, which, like the medieval epoch, can be divided into three periods. When classical antiquity celebrated its rebirth in Italy and then in the European countries beyond the Alps, an age began in which classical education and higher education in general became identical concepts. The ancients were regarded as the source from which intellectual cultivation was to be drawn, in substance as well as in form; the highest aspiration was to think, speak, and write as the ancients did, especially the Romans. And whereas nowadays people like to modernise antiquity—that is, to transfer modern conceptions, terms, and relationships into ancient conditions in order to bring them within the reach of modern understanding—it was then customary, especially in the country that was the cradle of humanism, to make the modern world antique: to introduce ancient concepts and institutions into modern conditions so as to reshape these entirely after their pattern. These Renaissance endeavours brought people into <!-- PDF_PAGE: 5 --> sharp opposition to the previous intellectual outlook and its medium, the Latin adapted to its needs. They became all the more conscious of this opposition in the latter respect because, passing over the Latin they recognised and ridiculed as barbarous, they did not take up the Latin of the fourth or fifth century, but returned to the classical period proper. Indeed, in Italy, where there was a particularly receptive feeling for beauty of form, a movement arose which, from among the exemplary writers, chose the master of classical prose style, Cicero, as its sole model for imitation [^5a]. Yet the reign of this enthusiastic humanism, beginning and ending at different times in the various cultured countries, was not of long duration. On average, it extended from the middle of the fifteenth to the middle of the sixteenth century. Humanism stopped halfway. Halted and confined by the ecclesiastical movements that came to the forefront of intellectual interest in the age of the Reformation, which enlisted it in its service, it failed to incorporate Greek antiquity, the true home of the classical world, into contemporary cultural life to the same extent as Roman antiquity, and thus to awaken, purify, and ennoble on every side the sense for what is truly and enduringly beautiful. That age simply did not discover the formula for bringing the human ideal of antiquity into harmony with what is truly Christian. The cultivation of classical Latin, entirely justified in itself, was not adequately sustained over time or harmoniously complemented by the cultivation of classical Greek. It degenerated into a one-sidedness that inevitably stunted the very nature of humanism, since humanism finds its vital element only in the union of Greek and Roman culture. Nevertheless, in the history of writing Latin, the humanism of that age has the imperishable merit of having reopened an understanding of the beautiful forms of antiquity and awakened the ability to reproduce them in literary works, so that henceforth the language seems secure against forcible distortion into barbarism.

The stunting of humanism, evident in German schools and life from the second half of the sixteenth century onwards, also affected the writing of Latin. Although the authentic form of ancient expression, as the Italians had recovered it, continued to be regarded as an ideal to be approached, and at least individual writers still wrote in a classical manner and received recognition for their efforts, Latin style in general nevertheless bore the stamp of its age. In Germany, the period from the second half of the sixteenth century to the middle of the eighteenth was, in general, one abandoned by the Muses and Graces, and Latin accordingly shared in all the lapses of taste from which that period suffered. In addition, as the exclusive medium of learned communication, the language was used by many who had only an inadequate command of it; it relapsed into the service of necessity and thus once again assumed a very unpleasing form. But compared with the flourishing age of humanism, Latin lost not only elegance within its own domain but also ground outside it. Around the middle of the sixteenth century, nearly 70 per cent of the books published in Germany were still written in Latin and only about 30 per cent in German, the largest contribution to the latter figure coming from Protestant polemical and devotional writings—Protestant theological scholarship used Latin almost exclusively. Even at the end of that century, Latin literature still accounted for 66 per cent and German for 34 per cent. During the seventeenth century, however, the German proportion rose steadily, so that in its final decade it already exceeded the Latin (54 per cent German, 44 Latin, 2 French). In the eighteenth century, the Latin proportion fell very rapidly: Latin's absolute dominion was coming to an end[^6a]. In the preceding period, Latin had been <!-- PDF_PAGE: 7 --> the common medium of educated people generally; in this one it had been restricted to the medium of scholars. Educated people, the higher social classes, turned to French.

From the middle of the eighteenth century and in the subsequent period (the nineteenth century), Latin style gains in purity and classical colouring in connection with the advance of classical scholarship brought about by the rediscovery of what is truly classical in the ancient, and especially the Greek, world. This improvement is due to eminent representatives of philological scholarship such as M. Gesner, Joh. Aug. Ernesti, Fr. Aug. Wolf, Gottfr. Hermann, and a number of their pupils, whose influence continues, directly and indirectly, down to the present day, with varying success, in philological literature and in the preparatory teaching of Latin at school. But the circle of those who write Latin grows ever narrower. In the last decade of the eighteenth century, books written in Latin account for scarcely more than 6 per cent of total production, as against about 89 per cent for German literature and approximately 5 per cent for French books published in Germany. In general, Latin ceases to be necessary for scholarly exposition. The mother tongue now suffices for this. Indeed, it not only suffices for scholarship but soon appears to be its only appropriate medium. Hand in hand with the powerful and seemingly independent development of the modern mind, it unfolds a wealth of means of expression with which the ancient language can no longer keep pace. Scholarship deposits all its riches in the German language. As a means of scholarly communication, Latin has become dispensable, indeed, as people say, unusable. The present age rejects writing Latin as pedantry; German scholarship, it is said, has pronounced sentence against it. Yet the question is whether stylistics can make itself necessary to this scholarship, indeed establish itself as an integral part of it.

§ 3 The answer to this question depends on a preliminary one: what was the position of stylistics in each of the main periods? In the first, understandably, it scarcely exists; whatever instruction of a stylistic kind was given in medieval schools consisted of grammatical and rhetorical precepts[^8a]. In the second main period, the predominant endeavour is initially to secure the newly recovered possession in its _purity_ and _completeness_, and subsequently also to counteract the corruption that is once again gaining ground. Hence the first two subdivisions of this period chiefly see the appearance of Antibarbari, phrase-books, and florilegia of every kind. The principal work was written around 1444 by the zealous guardian of newly awakened humanism, _Laurentius Valla_ (Lorenzo della Valle, 1407–1457). Under the title <span lang="la"><i>de linguae latinae elegantiis libri sex</i></span>, in which he sought to carry out his principle <span lang="la">‘ego pro lege accipio quidquid magnis auctoribus placuit’</span>, it circulated in 59 editions between 1471 and 1536[^8b]. _Desiderius Erasmus_, too, exercised a profound influence in the field of Latin style. He not only made Valla's work more generally accessible in the cultured countries beyond the Alps through an abridgement (<span lang="la"><i>Epitome in Elegantiarum libros L. Vallae</i></span>), but also intervened on his own account with his <span lang="la"><i>De duplici copia verborum ac rerum commentarii duo</i></span>, published in London in 1512, reprinted with extraordinary frequency thereafter, and still being adapted in the seventeenth century; also with his <span lang="la"><i>De ratione conscribendi epistolas</i></span> (1520, 1522), <span lang="la"><i>Antibarbara</i></span> (1522), and many other works chiefly concerned with the methods<!-- PDF_PAGE: 9; original word break: Me-|thodik --> of teaching (Bursian, loc. cit., p. 146). § 4 But these books contain no stylistics; they merely prepare the way for it, partly negatively, by eliminating barbarisms, partly positively, by supplying materials. A proper theory of style distinct from rhetoric is first outlined in a creditable manner (Hand, p. 17) in <span lang="la"><i>Jo. Ludov. Praschii Rosetum seu praecepta stili latini.</i></span> Ratisbon. 1676; <span lang="la"><i>cura G. Nicolai Kriegk.</i></span> Jena 1702. In this little book the cultivated jurist Prasche develops sound views on the concept of Latin style and on the way to attain it. Style, he holds, does not depend on grammatical correctness alone, for <span lang="la"><i>'orationem grammatice quidem rectam, ceterum rudem, indigestam, insolentem, denique informem nemo stilum, opinor, dixerit'</i></span> (<span lang="la"><i>ed. Kriegk.</i></span>, p. 9). Nor does rhetorical embellishment suffice, necessary though it may be. Style is the form of expression that accords with the way good Latin writers express their thoughts; what matters, therefore, is _color_, and specifically _color sanus_ (pp. 12–14). The book might have acquired importance if the bad taste of that age had paid it closer attention <!-- PDF_PAGE: 12 -->. Instead, because it swam with the current of the age, the greatest influence fell to <span lang="la"><i>Jo. Gottl. Heineccii stili cultioris fundamenta. Francof. 1719; edit. VII</i></span>, prepared by _Gesner_, <span lang="la"><i>Lips. 1743</i></span>; frequently reprinted down to 1790. This book, extravagantly praised on its appearance[^12a] and not entirely rejected even by _Fr. Aug. Wolf_[^12b], but called a <span lang="la"><i>liber futilis</i></span> by _Ruhnken_ (<span lang="la"><i>Epp. ad Ernesti ed. Tittm. Lips. 1812 p. 33</i></span>), undoubtedly did more harm than good. Although _Heinecke_, coming from the systematic school of jurists—his last position was as <span lang="la"><i>Professor iuris et philosophiae</i></span> at Halle—sought to give the theory of style a more rigorous course than it had previously followed, and wished to break with the practice of assembling isolated observations under merely external headings, he did not possess the purity of taste needed to combat the age's craving for so-called elegances. For all the phrase-books and Antibarbari were powerless to check the bad taste that swept over Germany after the advance of the mother tongue during the Reformation had been engulfed in the horrors of the Thirty Years' War. People sought protection against the coarseness spread by the war partly in Latin and partly in French elegance; the combination of these with the nation's awkwardness at the time produced a strange hybrid language ('Cäsar spoliirte den publiquen Tresor'—“Caesar despoiled the public treasury”; 'das Vaterland ist unser aller Papa'—“the fatherland is papa to us all”; and the like). _Elegant_ became the watchword of the stylists, and, since the feeling for truth and nature had been lost, the rare, affected, and unnatural very soon came to be called elegant. It is out of this taste that _Heinecke_ writes and teaches, and <!-- PDF_PAGE: 13 --> in turn helps to establish it in the schools. He recommends <span lang="la"><i>voces</i></span> and <span lang="la"><i>phrases elegantiores</i></span>, <span lang="la"><i>constructiones rariores</i></span>, p. 341, <span lang="la"><i>phrases selectiores</i></span>, p. 348, further <span lang="la"><i>ingeniosa judicia vel acumina</i></span>, p. 345, and the like; and in a panegyric on the jurist _Stryck_, appended to the book, he writes, as we read on p. 444: <span lang="la"><i>Stryckius — ex Anglia solvit secundoque ventorum flatu in Bataviam appulit. Alongside the pursuit of such phrases went the cultivation of three rhetorical arts, <span lang="la"><i>imitatio, variatio, dilatatio</i></span>, which were not so much objectionable in themselves as harmful through the abuse that pushed them to the most repellent artificiality. As regards <span lang="la"><i>imitatio</i></span>[^14a], it was customary (cf. _Heinecke_, p. 366) to recast some piece of writing in Ciceronian, Livian, Tacitean, or Plinian style. We have seen some of its fruits in that specimen from the panegyric on Stryck. It is remarkable that even _Wolf_ (Encyclop., p. 204) still recommends this transformation of styles. But the worst excesses were committed with <span lang="la"><i>variatio</i></span> and <span lang="la"><i>dilatatio</i></span>. Already in his work <span lang="la"><i>de duplici copia verborum</i></span>, cited above, _Erasmus_ varies the little sentence ‘<span lang="la"><i>semper, dum vivam, tui meminero</i></span>’ over more than ten closely printed octavo pages. And in his <span lang="la"><i>Compendiaria methodus addiscendi tres praecipuas latinae linguae virtutes, puritatem, elegantiam et copiam</i></span>, Diling. 1736, pp. 88 ff., the Jesuit _Wagner_ still copies out this feat as a model two centuries later. This is hardly surprising, however, since there was even a literature of its own devoted to this favourite branch of rhetorical and stylistic school exercises, and _Heinecke_ too advocated not only <span lang="la"><i>imitatio</i></span> but also <span lang="la"><i>variatio</i></span> and <span lang="la"><i>dilatatio</i></span>[^14b]. _Erasmus_ likewise provides remarkable examples of <span lang="la"><i>dilatatio</i></span> in the second book of that work, of which we shall reproduce the shortest (p. 238): <span lang="la"><i>Totus commaduit. A summo</i></span> <!-- PDF_PAGE: 15 --> <span lang="la"><i>capillo ad imum usque calcaneum pluvia commaduit. Caput, humeri, pectus, venter, tibiae, totum denique corpus pluvia distillabat.</i></span>

§ 5 While these largely unprofitable arts were being practised in the schools, and Latin lost its dignity to such an extent that before the middle of the eighteenth century Germany could scarcely boast more than three stylists of good taste, _Mosheim_, _Gesner_, and _Ernesti_, the mother tongue, drawing in part upon the language of the Church and the Bible, which had remained pure, rose on the other hand to an undreamt-of capacity for expression. German philosophy in particular put so many new concepts and words into circulation that Latin, as it had hitherto been studied and practised, no longer sufficed. Ernesti already felt this when, in his aforementioned preface to Weißmann's dictionary, he said: “There are, admittedly, especially in the present German manner of speaking (1758), many expressions that an inexperienced person will scarcely be able to translate correctly”; and he went on to complain “of the words and turns of phrase recently hatched in the German schools and writings of the philosophers, or smuggled into our language through clumsy translations from French and other languages.” Yet Ernesti knew no philosophy other than Wolff's, which he sought to clothe in Latin in his <span lang="la"><i>initiis solidioris doctrinae</i></span>, with such success that Gesner, in his preface to Vorst (see above, p. 10), calls that book a <span lang="la"><i>aureum opusculum</i></span>, <span lang="la"><i>ediscendum his, si qui philosophiae veteri et novae ita operam dare velint, ut ab latini sermonis puritate et naturali pulchritudine non recedant.</i></span>

With the development of German philosophy[^15a], whose individual schools exerted a powerful influence on the enrichment of the vocabulary, and with the ever-increasing strength imparted to our language by the poets, whose mode of expression soon also elevated and ennobled prose, writing Latin became more difficult in the same measure as it seemed to be becoming dispensable. It was no longer enough to achieve what Gesner, Ernesti, and their pupils had striven for: command of the genuinely Latin vocabulary as such, and mastery of Latin on its own terms<!-- PDF_PAGE: 16; original word break: be-|herrschen -->. One had to have it at one's disposal in relation to the riches developed within the German language; one had to know how the abundance of German diction, the multitude of demands made especially by scholarly style, could be met by the relative poverty of Latin expression. Here there was abundance, there scarcity. Hence the question arose: how can the means of expression sparingly allotted to the Latin writer suffice to meet the demands of German? It was necessary to investigate whether Latin, despite its comparative poverty, might be able to heighten the power of its resources; whether appropriate and vigorous use could bring what was available into a proper relation to what was needed; whether close attention might discover powers and means of expression in Latin that had never yet been called upon. This was the task that the development of the German language had set for a Latin stylistics for Germans.

An attempt to solve this problem yielded a comparison of languages of the utmost importance. If great value is rightly attached to comparative etymology, which nevertheless considers only the body of the word, and to comparative syntax, which considers only the relations and combinations of words, then surely one must acknowledge the scholarly legitimacy of an inquiry that takes, so to speak, the soul of language as its object, and brings within its scope all the powers through which thoughts are shaped and embodied in the two languages being compared. Here sound is not compared with sound, or grammatical government with grammatical government, but conception with conception, form of thought with form of thought, image with image, organism with organism. Two languages wrestle with one another; each measures its strength against its opponent, and through the demands made by the latter becomes increasingly aware of the resources at its own disposal. Indeed, the full extent of these resources comes properly to light only when unlike languages measure themselves against one another—not a rich language against another rich one, nor a modern language against another modern one, since both lend themselves too readily to interchange and accommodation. Thus no language is better suited than Latin to a fruitful contest with German. Through the exertions it must make to meet the demands of German, none gives a more vivid <!-- PDF_PAGE: 17 --> insight into the treasures of its _opponent_; in none does the contest bring its own powers more fully to light. Let the German give up writing Latin, and he simultaneously gives up a knowledge, to be gained from the contrast, of what makes his language so rich, and an ever-increasing understanding of the powers available in Latin. Let no one object that translation into the mother tongue is sufficient to acquire such insight. In that case we are merely prompted to match the achievements of a poor language with the resources of a rich one; we are only obliged to make ourselves thoroughly at home in our own possessions. But we do not learn, or learn only inadequately, the powers through which Latin poverty holds its own against German wealth; we do not draw upon the full resources of Latin, nor is it to Latin that we put the questions and problems.

§ 6 Thus the very task that a stylistics appropriate to our age must set itself already contains a justification of writing Latin; for no one disposed to adopt the standpoint of linguistic comparison will be able to deny its scholarly value. From this standpoint we regard stylistic endeavours as unassailable, however fully we concede that writing Latin is no longer an end in itself, and that this language can no longer be the exclusive instrument of scholarly communication. On the latter point, however, we cannot refrain from saying that people not infrequently entertain the strangest notions of Latin's inadequacy for modern scholarship, and often, through lack of knowledge of the subject, lay upon the language the blame that belongs to themselves. What is thought clearly and correctly can also be said in Latin. Grand phrases, admittedly, often cease to look grand when translated into Latin; but that is no fault of Latin. The opportunity it offers, like every language, to conceal poverty of thought behind fine diction is amply compensated for by its compelling lofty minds to strip the tinsel from their thoughts and present them in their nakedness. From the standpoint of linguistic comparison it is also easy to show that proficiency in speaking and writing Latin, although it no longer makes the scholar, is nevertheless still an ornament of learning. For learning has no true value without cultivation; and cultivation is inconceivable unless, in brief, one <!-- PDF_PAGE: 18 --> understands the workings of the mind and can recognise it in the activity through which it expresses itself most directly and purely. But no one can do this who speaks his own language merely instinctively, like a child, without acquainting himself with the different forms in which the mind finds expression among different peoples; nor can anyone who stops at the forms most closely related to his own language, without engaging with those essentially and originally different from it. Finally, from that standpoint, writing Latin in school is justified. The school's task is to cultivate the mind, not only by introducing it to its most splendid productions, but also by showing what the mind is in itself, in its most immediate and accessible activity, language. To show this, it likewise needs to set out the different forms in which the mind manifests itself among the foremost cultured nations.

§ 7 If we now ask how stylistics has responded to the historically developed demands of scholarship in our century, we cannot deny that, despite the most thoroughgoing and substantial improvements in detail, as a whole and in its method it has remained at the standpoint it adopted a hundred years ago. For until the most recent times it did not advance beyond the division current in the previous century, according to which one dealt first with the correctness and then with the beauty of Latin style. In his first part Heinecke speaks <span lang="la"><i>de fundamentis stili grammaticis, rhetoricis, philosophicis</i></span>; of the first two he says (p. 198) that they <span lang="la"><i>ad orationis structuram et ornatum</i></span>, and of the last that they <span lang="la"><i>ad orationis materiam pertinent</i></span>. We have here nothing other than, by his own admission on p. 55, an incomplete grammar, then the <span lang="la"><i>locus de elocutione</i></span> from rhetoric, and finally a meagre outline of logic. His other two parts deal, the second <span lang="la"><i>de variis scriptionibus in soluta oratione usitatis</i></span>, the third <span lang="la"><i>de variis cultioris stili facultatem assequendi subsidiis</i></span>, and thus do not touch the art of expression at all. The Jesuit _Wagner_, mentioned above, treats in his <span lang="la"><i>compendiaria methodus</i></span>: 1) <span lang="la"><i>de puritate</i></span>, 2) <span lang="la"><i>de elegantia</i></span>, 3) <span lang="la"><i>de copia sermonis</i></span>. If we consult a work on stylistics from the beginning of the present century, we find in <!-- PDF_PAGE: 19 --> <span lang="la"><i>Hasse de causis linguae latinae</i></span> (1802) the divisions 1) <span lang="la"><i>de oratione grammatica</i></span>, 2) <span lang="la"><i>de elegantia</i></span>, 3) <span lang="la"><i>de ornatu</i></span>; in Hand's predecessor, _August Matthiä_, Entwurf einer Theorie des lateinischen Stils, Leipzig 1826, style is treated according to its “essential requirements”: “correctness, clarity, agreeableness, and appropriateness.” Finally, in _Ferdinand Hand's_ excellent and highly instructive work (2nd edition 1839; 3rd edition revised by Dr. _Heinr. Ludw. Schmitt_, Jena 1880), after general and historical discussions we find 1. Principles of Latin style with regard to correctness, 2. Principles of Latin style with regard to beauty. This is also the basic division of Heinichen's Lehrbuch der Theorie des lateinischen Stils, 2nd edition, Leipzig 1848, and essentially that of _Reinhold Kloß_ in his Handbuch der lateinischen Stilistik, edited by _Richard Kloß_, Leipzig, Teubner 1874. Only in more recent times has _Moritz Seyffert_ in particular worked in practice, in his Palästra Ciceroniana, in the spirit of linguistic comparison as we understand it[^19a]; and _F. J. Grysar_, too, in the 2nd edition of his Theorie des lateinischen Stiles (Cologne 1843), had in view the goal we envisage, although his treatment of the subject differs essentially from ours[^19b]. The sixth edition of Krebs-Allgayer's Antibarbarus der lateinischen Sprache was revised by _F. H. Schmalz_ (Basel 1886–1888) in the spirit of comparison between Latin and German; in his hands the book has become a guide as reliable as it is indispensable for the teacher of Latin style. On his historical stylistics, see below, p. 25 [^19c].

If our discussions are correct, the fault in the method hitherto followed is self-evident. We leave aside the fact that many a recent work on stylistics would look exceedingly bare if grammar and rhetoric reclaimed the material borrowed from them—indeed, to mention only older works, K. L. Bauer's Anleitung zum richtigen und guten Ausdruck in der lat. Spr., Breslau 1775 (1796), and also <i>Scheller's</i> well-known <span lang="la">Praecepta stili bene latini</span>, Lips. 1779 (1793), are nothing more than grammars. But present-day stylistics fails to take account of the German's principal need, arising from the development of our language into a wealth of expressive resources that Latin no longer seems able to match. For even where the choice of words and turns of phrase is discussed, the aim is far more to eliminate what is faulty than to open up the resources of the vocabulary. Latin continues to be considered solely in relation to itself, with linguistic comparison undertaken only incidentally, and without meeting the practical need of the learner, who can learn correctness of style from grammar but cannot possibly meet the requirements of a beautiful style as long as he lacks what is most essential: the means of expression.

We therefore assign the theory of correctness, in so far as it discusses matters taught in syntax, to grammar, and the theory of beauty of style, in so far as it applies to style in every language, to rhetoric. We take our stand midway between grammar and rhetoric and inquire, generally without regard to particular kinds of style, <!-- PDF_PAGE: 21 --> into the relation of the means of expression in Latin to those in German[^21a].

§ 8 Since, as we have seen, writing Latin is out of the question unless the German knows how, as a Latin stylist, he can meet the demands of the richness of German expression, the first part of a comparative stylistics must be a Topics (_Topik_): an indication of the places where the Latin expression corresponding to the German <!-- PDF_PAGE: 22 --> is to be sought. These Topics seek to show the German, within the Latin language, the means of expression equivalent to the newly developed wealth of his own language. If they are not found ready-made among the language's materials, but only in their essential possibilities within grammatical structures, then grammar too enters into stylistics: a power asserts itself in the grammatical relation that takes the place of a word which is either materially absent or cannot readily be adapted to appropriate use.

But since languages differ chiefly in the means by which they give expression light and shade, or its appropriate colouring, the Topics require as their necessary complement a _Tropics_ (_Tropik_), or, properly, a _Metaphorics_ (_Metaphorik_), as we should say if the expression were customary: that is, a theory of the relation between metaphors in the two languages. Tropics cannot be placed alongside Topics as an independent main part, but constitutes the second subdivision of Topics itself, since it teaches us to recognise the <span lang="la"><i>Translata</i></span> as opposed to the <span lang="la"><i>Propria</i></span>. The reason why Tropics is restricted to the theory of metaphor, whereas synecdoche and metonymy must be included in the account of the <span lang="la"><i>Propria</i></span>, lies in the difference between these figures themselves. Of them, metaphor alone gives prose the colouring mentioned above; the other two, by contrast, supply substitutes for missing <span lang="la"><i>Propria</i></span> in the sphere of expression without imagery.

In these two sections, however, the materials of language are as yet considered only in their inorganic aspect, in so far as they consist of words and expressions. But the means of expression at a language's disposal also include the forms of sentences and periods possible within it, their structure and connection—in short, the organic formations it produces. The second main part of stylistics will therefore compare the two languages with regard to the construction of their discourse, and for this reason we call it _Architectonics_ (_Architektonik_).

The foregoing principles of Nägelsbach, on which the teaching that follows is based, were described as one-sided by J. Müßell in the Ztsch. f. <!-- PDF_PAGE: 23 --> Gymn. I (1847), p. 164, immediately after the appearance of the first edition of the Stilistik (1846), and more recently by Fr. Aug. Eckstein (Lat. u. Gr. Unterr., p. 316; cf. Schmid's Encykl. XI, p. 674). Even if this is conceded, it would nevertheless be unjust to deny Nägelsbach's standpoint a legitimate place in the theory of Latin style, or to accord it only subordinate importance. One must bear in mind that Latin style can be treated from different sides, all of which must be recognised as equally legitimate, and that a theoretical method capable of satisfying every requirement will scarcely be found. Thus probably every mode of treatment must bear the stamp of one-sidedness from the outset.

I\. If one starts from the concept and properties of style in the verbal arts, particularly in prose—that is, from the thought that the artistic form of linguistic expression is subject to the fundamental laws of artistic expression in general—then the theory of Latin style will have to show in detail in what manner and to what degree these fundamental laws have been realised in the products of Roman prose literature. And if the chief law of all artistic expression consists in the harmonious union of the fundamental idea of truth with that of beauty, the former leading to the requirement of linguistic and logical correctness, the latter to the requirement of beauty of expression, then there can be no doubt that, viewed from that standpoint, the development of all the properties that follow from these two fundamental stylistic requirements must be the subject of the theory of style in general, and their application specifically to artistic Latin the subject of the theory of Latin style. As for the theory of linguistic correctness, since its object, <span lang="la"><i>locutio emendata et Latina</i></span>, consists not merely in the choice and use of genuinely Latin words and turns of phrase, but also in the inflection, connection, and arrangement of words in accordance with the language, it ought, strictly speaking, also to include the field now assigned to grammar. But since grammar has emancipated itself from the theory of style, and to an even greater degree than the latter has from rhetoric, the theory of linguistic correctness usually presupposes a <!-- PDF_PAGE: 24 --> knowledge of grammar or refers to it, although the boundaries between grammar and this part of the theory of style cannot be sharply drawn, because their fields overlap (_Schmalz_, Hdb. II, pp. 365–366). We set aside any further division of the subject matter arising from the restricted concept of linguistic correctness and from the concept of logical and aesthetic correctness, since our concern here is only to sketch general points of view. We bring together under the name of the **objective theory of Latin style** the theory that considers the Latin mode of expression in itself, without paying special attention to the features distinguishing it from other languages, and measures the properties of Latin style against the general laws of art, as well as against the national character of the Roman people and their degree of cultivation. Its scholarly founder is _Ferdinand Hand_. His theory is the systematic, rational synthesis of everything that, since Laurentius Valla, had either hovered indistinctly before the mind as a theory of style or had been grasped externally and mechanically on the basis of isolated empirical observations. The first edition of his Lehrbuch des lat. Stils, Jena 1833, marks the beginning of the epoch in which the objective theory of style was placed on a scholarly foundation.

If the ultimate aim of such a theory is to understand how far the distinctive national character and cultivation of the Romans are reflected in the artistic expression of thought, then the theory must naturally encompass first and foremost that cultural period in which the Roman world of thought attained its most perfect and purest verbal expression, and that union of the virtues of correctness and beauty which constitutes the classical character of style was achieved: the period, therefore, in which prose style developed into its classical form. Now differences in the subject matter of thought to be expressed, together with the purpose to be achieved, entail differences in the form of thought. The theory will therefore also have to consider the different kinds of style that developed within the classical period—historical, oratorical, scholarly, and so forth. If these genres of prose are treated separately, distinct theories of historical, oratorical, didactic<!-- PDF_PAGE: 25; original word break: didak=|tischen --> prose, and so forth, emerge as independent branches of the general objective theory of Latin style: tasks that have not yet been accomplished in philological literature. If, on the other hand, attention is directed to the way a writer invests the expression of his thoughts with his individuality, and to how far he submits to the general laws of artistic expression, further branches emerge in the form of studies of the style of a particular writer—tasks that, although undertaken, still await a comprehensive solution.

But the model period to which the objective method of stylistic theory must adhere as its κανών itself stands in the midst of the stream of development followed by Roman culture. A result of preceding efforts at cultivation and a point of departure for subsequent ages, it nevertheless had to give way to other stylistic aspirations and tendencies of taste belonging to the spirit of the time. If we now consider the changes to which prose style was subject over the centuries, and work out in detail how far the different ages complied with the general laws of style and preserved their national stamp, down to the disappearance of literary Latin, we arrive at the concept and task of historical stylistics. This task follows necessarily from the method of historical grammar undertaken in more recent times; the merit of having attempted its first solution belongs to Headmaster _J. H. Schmalz_ of Tauberbischofsheim. His “Lateinische Stilistik” in the editor's Handbuch der klassischen Altertumswissenschaft, vol. II, Nördlingen 1885, pp. 365–411, marks the beginning of the epoch in which historical stylistic theory was placed on a scholarly foundation. Various independent branches can also be conceived within this method of treatment. It may, for example, become particularly fruitful for the developmental history of the different kinds of style; and only through it will a detailed consideration of the individual styles of writers who give a particular literary period its stamp acquire its proper background.

II\. The treatment will assume a character different from that of the objective method, whether systematic or historical, if it takes as its subject the comparison of the peculiarities of Latin style <!-- PDF_PAGE: 26 --> with those of another language. The most natural course for the author of a comparative stylistics is to measure the peculiarities of the foreign style against his mother tongue. The scholarly founder of the comparison of Latin expression with German is _Nägelsbach_. Although, as Müßell reports <span lang="la"><i>l. l.</i></span>, p. 169, he had some predecessors, and practical school exercises increasingly led to such a comparison as German was increasingly made the basis of instruction, the methodical execution of comparative stylistics dates only from 1846, the year when Nägelsbach's Stilistik first appeared. In a similar spirit, <span lang="la"><i>Antonio Cima</i></span> treated the theory of Latin style for Italian in his <span lang="la"><i>Principii della stilistica latina, Milano</i></span>, 1881 (cf. also his <span lang="la"><i>Breve teoria dello stile, Torino</i></span>, 1883). We know of no independent treatments, separated from grammar and conceived in the comparative spirit, from other cultured countries. A parallel stylistics of classical Latin and classical Greek would certainly also be a worthwhile undertaking. Such a comparison, already an attractive subject for the Romans, would, if pursued with the methods of the most recent times, make a substantial contribution to understanding the stylistic peculiarities of the two cultured peoples of antiquity, and at the same time lead to a thorough knowledge of the differences between ancient and modern style in general [^26a].

The comparative treatment can take different forms, whatever language it starts from. It can become a combination with the method of the objective theory, and it was in this sense that _H. L. Schmitt_ sought to reshape Hand's theory. But it can also place its chief emphasis on opening up the resources of Latin vocabulary to modern writers, finding its principal task in showing how far Latin expression suffices to meet the demands of modern concepts and conceptions. For German, this is _Nägelsbach's_ standpoint. The need to approach Latin stylistics from this standpoint became all the more pressing as German language and literature grew richer <!-- PDF_PAGE: 27 --> and the modern mind moved further away from the ancient. See above, pp. 16 ff., and Nägelsbach's preface. And because this mode of treatment met an undeniable need of our time, it achieved great success and cannot be considered dispensable for the future either[^27a], although Latin stylistics is here confined within narrower limits than some might wish. But this arises from the restriction of the subject matter entailed by the adopted standpoint, and is not peculiar to Nägelsbach's method.

For although the objective and comparative theories do not exclude one another, each nevertheless allows the other's points of view to recede into the background, so that these cannot receive their full due. Objective systematics, rooted in the classical period, cannot avoid casting a glance at the stylistic peculiarities of the preceding and subsequent ages; but it does so only incidentally and fragmentarily, and thus fails to satisfy the historical and genetic approach. The latter, on the other hand, will forgo the advantages that a systematic treatment can offer for the aesthetic understanding of classical Latin. In both methods, however, attention to the differences between Latin and another cultivated language recedes, even if these are touched upon or tacitly presupposed, although an exact knowledge of them is indispensable both to the writer of Latin and to the interpreter. Conversely, the comparative method leaves much of what the systematic method emphasises and the historical method carries out unconsidered, although it is by no means closed to what constitutes the principal subject of both. Thus its chief representative, when discussing Latin's means and powers of expression in relation to modern German, did not, in order to show how far the genius of Latin could go and what it might permit itself, confine himself to the model Latin of the classical epoch. He also drew upon preclassical and postclassical usage, especially the style of <!-- PDF_PAGE: 28 --> Livy, for his purposes—fragmentarily, admittedly, but no differently from the way objective treatments handle the comparative element.

III\. People nowadays speak of the “stylistics of the future.” In the light of what precedes, the future task of the different treatments of stylistics, apart from those serving pedagogical purposes, cannot consist in one approach seeking to displace another and setting itself up—perhaps through the artifice of a sham eclecticism—as the only legitimate one. It must consist instead in each consistently carrying through, within clearly drawn limits, the principles that follow from its adopted standpoint[^28a]. Depending on the scholarly currents of the time, one tendency may perhaps be cultivated in preference to another. But a temporary preference by no means implies that this tendency should be regarded as the stylistics of the future κατ’ ἐξοχήν, any more than Wagner's music, currently favoured and far-reaching in its influence, can be regarded as the absolute music of the future. The true “stylistics of the future” will rather be a harmonious union of all the endeavours proceeding alongside one another to illuminate the nature of ancient Latin from the most diverse sides and thereby achieve a comprehensive and fruitful understanding of it. And such a stylistics will continue to exist so long as those truly culture-hostile powers that wish to drive Latin and Greek, or either of them, out of the education of young people destined for higher cultivation do not gain the upper hand and thereby cut the thread of life of the future of classical studies altogether.

[^21a]: The German–Latin dictionaries to which one might perhaps refer naturally do not, in so far as they overlap with our purpose, treat the principles of the matter, and thus provide no scholarly insight whatever into the difference between the languages. Serious objections can also be raised against them from a pedagogical standpoint. Here, moreover, we must justify why in general we take no account of particular kinds of style. Given the task we have set ourselves, this seems to us unavoidable. We are to supply the stylist with means of expression, and that is what we intend to do. Considered in themselves, however, these are, in the vast majority of cases, the same for all kinds of prose style—and these alone concern us—with the sole exception of tropes. The letter-writer, the historian, the orator, and the philosopher differ not in having different verbal materials, but in making different uses of those materials. Good and classical expressions can be used, in the right place and an appropriate context, in every kind of style; cf. <span lang="la"><i>Cic. or.</i> 3, 45, 177</span>; <span lang="la"><i>Quintil.</i> 10, 1, 9</span>. What makes the style historical, philosophical, and so forth is not the individual word or the individual structure, but the purpose and thought governing the mutual relations of the words. Admittedly, where particular expressions have been given a special character once and for all by the language, as with some expressions of familiar speech, or are technical terms created exclusively for a particular discipline, or belong only to the Silver Age of Latinity, this is either evident from the sources from which we draw them or has been expressly noted by us. The same applies to the organic linguistic formations introduced by our Architectonics. We have only to show what forms of sentence and period are possible in the language. The selection of individual forms for a particular kind of style rests no longer on stylistic but on rhetorical understanding. If, in setting out the means of expression, we immediately took particular account of the kind of style, we should contradict ourselves by once again mixing together the disciplines we wish to separate.
